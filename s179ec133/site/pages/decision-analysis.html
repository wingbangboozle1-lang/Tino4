Live in it, rent it, or sell and reinvest

Three plans for Property A after it is inherited, compared after tax, with Property B kept in every case.

Result: for an inheritance in 2045 and a 20-year view, selling Property A and investing the proceeds leaves the household with the most wealth. Renting it out is second; living in it is third. Property B is kept in every plan.

All amounts on this page are after federal and California income tax, after selling costs, and — unless marked "then-year dollars" — in 2026 dollars. Each plan ends by turning everything into cash after 20 years so that the three can be compared like for like.

  • Keeping the house means paying tax on a value of millions. Even with the family exclusion, the yearly bill is about $49,910 in 2046 — more than four times the bill before inheritance.
  • As a rental it barely pays for itself. Rent of about $120,660 is 2.2% of the home's value; property tax, insurance and upkeep take most of it, leaving about $28,680.
  • Selling costs almost nothing in tax. The gain built up during the parents' ownership is never taxed, because the basis resets at death.
  • The growth then comes from the investment, not the house. The projection uses 7% a year for stocks against 3% a year for the house; the black-swan page tests what happens when those are wrong.

The plan

  1. Keep Property B, keep living in its third unit, keep the two rents.
  2. On inheriting Property A, order a date-of-death appraisal: it fixes the tax basis.
  3. Do not move in and do not let it — list it for sale. The county bills property tax at the reassessed value until it sells, so a quick sale saves money.
  4. Invest the net proceeds in a broad, low-cost stock index fund, keeping in Treasuries whatever the household may need within about five years.
  5. If the family would rather live in Property A, treat the difference on this page as the price, move in within one year and file the claims on Inheritance step by step.
