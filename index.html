Recommended: sell the Cupertino house after the step-up, keep the San Francisco building

Why. The Cupertino house costs about 1.2% of its value in property tax and produces a gross rent yield near 2%. A diversified portfolio compounding at 5.5% after tax beats that unless Cupertino appreciates faster than about 7.4% a year — or faster than about 5.5% if the portfolio only earns 4% after tax. Selling soon after death is the cheapest exit that will ever exist: the basis steps up to market value, so the capital gain is roughly zero, and no transfer tax is triggered by inheriting.

What it costs you. You give up the house, the Prop 19 base and the option to live there. Against the "live in it" path the model shows about $7,294,720 of additional 2065 net worth on a hold basis.

Two things to fix long before 2040

1. Make sure the Cupertino house is the parents' principal residence at death. Prop 19 relief is available only for the transferor's principal residence. Today the family lives in the Sunset building, so on the current facts the exclusion would be lost entirely — worth about $20,040 a year at 2045, and more later.

2. Decide before the one-year deadline, not after. The exclusion requires you to occupy the house within one year of the transfer and file the homeowners' exemption within that same year; the BOE-19-P claim itself has a three-year window. Renting the house out later ends the exclusion.

Executive summary — nine findings

  1. There is no death tax to plan around at these amounts. The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person for 2026 deaths and is indexed after that; California has no inheritance tax and no state estate tax. The exposure only begins if the total estate — houses plus cash — exceeds the indexed exclusion.
  2. The Cupertino house is assessed at ~18% of its market value. That ratio, not the house itself, is the asset being inherited. Any plan that triggers a reassessment destroys most of it.
  3. Prop 19 relief is conditional and only partial here. It requires the house to be the parents' principal residence at death and the heir's principal residence within a year. Even then, with a ~$550,000 base against a ~$6.0M value in 2045, it saves roughly $20,000 a year — not the whole reassessment.
  4. The one-year and three-year deadlines are the real risk. Occupy within 12 months and file the homeowners' exemption within 12 months; file BOE-19-P within 3 years. Miss either and the relief is reduced or lost.
  5. Selling shortly after death is the cheapest exit that will ever exist. The basis steps up to market value, so the gain is near zero, and an inheritance does not trigger transfer tax.
  6. Base case: sell the Cupertino house and reinvest. 2065 net worth of $29.8M against $25.4M for living in it and $25.3M for renting it out — a $4.4M margin on a hold basis, $7.3M if everything is liquidated.
  7. Living in it and renting it out are effectively tied. The $63,350 difference over 20 years means the occupancy decision can be made on lifestyle grounds rather than tax grounds.
  8. Keep the San Francisco building. Selling it as well drops 2065 net worth by $7.2M: its rent-controlled income on a $114,000 base is the best-yielding asset in the file.
  9. Caveat on the whole analysis. The ranking is a bet on appreciation versus portfolio returns. The house wins if Cupertino compounds above 7.4% while the portfolio earns 5.5% — or above 5.5% if the portfolio earns only 4%.
