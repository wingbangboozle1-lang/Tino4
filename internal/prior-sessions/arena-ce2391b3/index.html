The answer in one paragraph

Property A is a $3,100,000 single-family home assessed at only $539,743, which is why its tax bill is $7,207 a year instead of the $41,391 a year a buyer at today's value would pay. Proposition 19 lets that low assessment survive the inheritance — but only if someone moves in and stays in, and only for as long as they stay. The model says the low assessment is worth roughly $1,044,590 of taxable value and about $13,950 a year at the transfer date. Against that, Property A is a 3,100,000 dollar asset earning no income while you live in it, and a $9,817,780 asset by 2065 if it appreciates at the base-case 3%/yr. Over a 20-year horizon, measured on the same after-tax liquidation basis, Sell Property A and reinvest ends at $17,303,850, versus $11,733,730 for renting it out and $10,411,360 for living in it.

That ordering flips if the reinvestment return falls materially below the base case, or if Property A appreciates faster than 3%/yr. Both break-evens are computed on the Decision analysis page rather than left as a claim.

Three strategies, same horizon, same tax treatment

All three are valued by notionally selling everything in 2065 and paying the capital-gains tax, with cash received along the way added back. No option wins merely by deferring tax.

Read this before relying on any number

  • The Property B tax figures imply a 2.14% effective rate. San Francisco's published secured rate is 1.1816%. That gap has persisted for ten years and is either real parcel-level charges or a data error. One actual tax bill settles it.
  • Insurance is the weakest input. Both figures are the user's own estimate. No official body publishes a parcel-level premium. Earthquake coverage is not included at all.
  • No 2026 property tax bill exists yet for Property A, so that row is blank rather than filled in.
  • The decision is sensitive to the reinvestment return. At the base case the sell case wins by a wide margin; at 4.5% instead of 6.5% the margin largely disappears. Break-even is about 4.5%.
