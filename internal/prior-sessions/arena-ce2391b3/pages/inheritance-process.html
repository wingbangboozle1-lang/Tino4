Inheritance process

Step by step from trust funding to the tax deadlines, with each rule cited to the agency that enforces it.

The sequence, with the deadlines that actually bite

Two deadlines in this process are unforgiving: the one-year homeowners' exemption claim, which is a precondition of the Prop 19 exclusion, and the three-year exclusion claim itself. Everything else can be fixed later. Those two cannot.

  1. Before the death

    Confirm the trust is funded

    A revocable trust only avoids probate for assets actually titled in it. Check that both properties and the financial accounts are titled in the trust's name, not still in the parents' individual names. If they are not, they pass through the will and probate instead, which in California is slow and expensive on a multi-million-dollar estate.

    No official source — general practice
    Flagged: Probate fee amounts are deliberately not estimated here — they depend on the statutory schedule and the estate's facts.
  2. Before the death

    Confirm which property is the principal residence

    Prop 19's parent-child exclusion applies only to the transferor's principal residence. If Property A is not the parents' principal residence for tax purposes, the exclusion is unavailable and the house is reassessed to market value. If the exclusion is unavailable, Property A is reassessed to market value and the property tax roughly triples, which is the whole difference between the live-in and rent-out cases.

    Source: California State Board of Equalization
    Flagged: Not stated in the request. Flagged F6.
  3. Before the death

    Consider the annual gift exclusion while it is available

    The 2026 annual gift exclusion is $19,000 per recipient per donor, or $38,000 for a married couple splitting gifts. Lifetime gifts reduce the taxable estate and do not touch the recipient's basis problem in the same way an inheritance does — but they also forfeit the step-up in basis on the gifted asset, so this is a genuine trade-off, not a free win.

    Source: Internal Revenue Service
  4. At death — month 0

    Trust administration begins

    The successor trustee takes control of trust assets. Real property is transferred to the beneficiaries by a trustee's deed, which is recorded with the county. No court filing is required for property that is actually in the trust.

    Source: Internal Revenue Service
  5. At death — month 0

    Establish the date-of-death value

    This is the number that matters for tax. For inherited property the heir's basis is the fair market value on the date of death Internal Revenue Service. Obtain a defensible appraisal as of that date. If the estate has to file a federal Form 706, the value on the return also binds the heir's basis under the consistent-basis rules.

    Source: Internal Revenue Service
  6. Within days

    File the Preliminary Change of Ownership Report

    A PCOR (BOE-502-A) is normally filed with the deed at recording. Missing it costs an extra $20 in San Francisco City and County of San Francisco and the same $20 in Santa Clara County Santa Clara County Office of the Clerk-Recorder. The county uses it to determine whether the transfer is a change in ownership.

    Source: Santa Clara County Office of the Clerk-Recorder
  7. Within days

    Record the deed; pay any documentary transfer tax

    Property A: Santa Clara County charges $0.55 per $500 of value Santa Clara County Office of the Clerk-Recorder. Property B: San Francisco charges per a whole-value bracket — $3.75 per $500 for $1M to under $5M City and County of San Francisco. A transfer from a trust to a beneficiary is typically exempt from transfer tax as a gift/no-consideration transfer, but the exemption must be claimed on the affidavit at recording. A sale is not exempt.

    Source: City and County of San Francisco
    Flagged: Whether the trust-to-heir transfer qualifies for an exemption depends on the trust terms and the affidavit. Confirm with the trustee's attorney before recording.
  8. Within 1 year — hard deadline

    Claim the homeowners' exemption on Property A

    File BOE-266 with the Santa Clara County Assessor. This $7,000 reduction in taxable value is worth about $93 a year at a 1.3358% rate. More importantly, under Prop 19 the exclusion itself is conditioned on a homeowners' or disabled-veterans' exemption being claimed within one year of the transfer. Miss it and the exclusion can be lost entirely, which is worth roughly $13,950 a year.

    Source: California State Board of Equalization
  9. Within 3 years — hard deadline

    File the Prop 19 parent-child exclusion claim (BOE-19-P)

    File with the Santa Clara County Assessor within three years of the transfer or before a transfer to a third party, whichever is earlier. Santa Clara County charges a $110 non-refundable processing fee Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor. The exclusion keeps the parent's factored base year value plus $1,044,586 of value, so for Property A the calculation is: parent's factored base year value on the transfer date, plus any amount by which market value exceeds that plus $1,044,586.

    Source: California State Board of Equalization
  10. Years 1–2

    Expect a supplemental tax bill

    A change in ownership triggers reappraisal. Where the new value is higher, the county issues a supplemental assessment for the difference, prorated for the part of the fiscal year remaining City and County of San Francisco. Budget for one or two of these in the first 18 months — they are easy to miss and they are not in the normal annual bill.

    Source: City and County of San Francisco
  11. If selling — year 1

    Sell and claim the stepped-up basis

    Basis is the date-of-death value, so a prompt sale at that value produces essentially no taxable gain. California withholds 3⅓% of the price at close California Franchise Tax Board; that is a prepayment, recovered on the California return, and the gain-based election on Form 593 usually reduces the holdback. If the heir instead lives in the property for two of the five years before selling, the federal $500,000 joint §121 exclusion becomes available on top Internal Revenue Service.

    Source: California Franchise Tax Board
    Flagged: California does not fully conform to §121. A gain sheltered federally can still carry California tax. Confirm with a CPA before relying on the shelter.
  12. If living in it — every year after

    Stay in it, or lose the exclusion

    The exclusion is conditional on continuous occupancy. Once Property A stops being the heir's principal residence, it is reassessed to the date-of-death fair market value, factored forward to the lien date following the move-out California State Board of Equalization. Living in it for a few years and then renting it out does not preserve the low base — the deferred reassessment catches up, and it catches up on a value that has grown.

    Source: California State Board of Equalization
    Flagged: This is the single most expensive mistake available in this decision.

What would make the whole process go wrong

  • The properties are not in the trust. Then it is probate, not trust administration.
  • Nobody moves in within the statutory window. The exclusion is simply not available, and Property A is reassessed to market value.
  • The exemption claim is late. Prop 19 conditions the exclusion on a timely homeowners' or disabled-veterans' exemption.
  • The heir moves out later. The deferred reassessment arrives at the date-of-death value, factored forward — which is higher by then.
  • Property A is rented out at the time of death. Then it is not the transferor's principal residence and Prop 19 does not apply at all.
