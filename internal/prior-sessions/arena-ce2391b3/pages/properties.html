Two things the supplied series cannot explain

1. Property B's implied tax rate is 2.14%, but San Francisco publishes 1.1816%

$2,440 ÷ $114,250 = 2.1350%. The published FY2026-27 secured rate is 1.18162182% City and County of San Francisco. The gap is roughly $1,070 a year, and it has persisted for a decade — the implied rate ranges from 1.68% (2016) to 2.14% (2025). A ten-year consistent gap is more likely to be real parcel-level charges (special assessments, bond levies, a district charge that is not in the secured rate) or a second parcel appearing on the same bill, than a typo. The model uses the implied 2.135% so the projection matches the bills actually paid, and shows the published rate beside it.

Action: one actual tax bill settles it.

2. Property A's implied rate is 1.335%, against a county illustration of 1.1488%

$7,210 ÷ $539,740 = 1.3352%. Santa Clara County's own illustration of a typical tax rate area is 1.1488% Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor. A 16% excess is explained easily by a tax rate area with more bond load, but it cannot be confirmed without the parcel number. The model uses the implied rate and runs the county figure as a sensitivity.

3. Two Property A years break the pattern

In 2017 the tax fell 0.5% while the assessment rose 2%. In 2020 the tax was identical to 2019 ($6,560) while the assessment rose 2%. Neither can be produced by a constant-rate model. They are kept exactly as supplied rather than corrected.
