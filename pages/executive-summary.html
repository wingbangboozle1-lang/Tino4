The important details in one place: what drives the answer, what the statutes actually require, and what would reverse the conclusion.

The finding in one paragraph

The single largest number in this decision is not the sale price and not the rent. It is the property tax step-up at the moment of transfer. Property A is modelled at $3,100,000 today and about $5,959,750 at the default transfer year of 2045. Under current rules the enrolled assessment moves from roughly $802,030 to about $4,407,540 if the home is occupied as a principal residence, or about $5,959,750 if it is not. That carries the annual bill from about $7,360 to about $59,560 or $80,660, a multiple of roughly 8.1x or 11.0x. Every other choice has to be measured against that step.

Property B is a different asset with different economics. It is already owned, it is the current residence, and two of its three units produce about $150,450 of gross rent a year on the owner's figures. Its enrolled assessment and therefore its bill are far lower relative to value. Property B is what makes a mixed path possible, because it lets the household keep rental income while changing how Property A is used.

Conditional recommendation

On the evidence available today, and only if the home is the transferor's principal residence at transfer, the path that preserves the most optionality is to take occupancy within the one-year window, file the homeowners' exemption, file the exclusion claim within three years, and only then decide whether to stay, rent or sell. That ordering preserves the reduced assessment while the market, the insurance market and the household's own needs become clearer. It does not require deciding now whether to keep the property for life.

If the home is not the transferor's principal residence at transfer, or if occupancy within one year is not realistic, the exclusion is likely unavailable. In that case the comparison narrows to a concentrated, low-yield asset with a heavy annual bill versus selling and reinvesting, and the answer becomes much more sensitive to the reinvestment path than to the property itself.

This is a conditional conclusion, not a recommendation to act. It reverses if the trust restricts occupancy, if insurance becomes unavailable at a workable price, if the estate is large enough to create a filing obligation, or if the household needs the proceeds.
