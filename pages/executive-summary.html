Executive summary

The recommendation, the three numbers that drive it, where it breaks, and what to do first.

1. The decision

Around 2045 (the stated window is 2040–2050, modelled at the midpoint) Property A — a single-family home in Cupertino, ZIP 95014 — passes to the heir through a trust, alongside a substantial cash amount. Property B, a three-unit building in San Francisco's Outer Sunset, ZIP 94122, is already owned and stays owned. The question is what to do with Property A:

  1. Live in it. Move in, claim the Prop 19 parent-child exclusion, keep Property B's rent.
  2. Rent it out. Stay in Property B, collect rent from both properties, lose the Prop 19 exclusion.
  3. Sell it. Sell shortly after inheritance at a stepped-up basis, reinvest the proceeds, keep Property B rented.

2. What the model found

On a common after-tax liquidation basis at 2065:

RankStrategyTerminal net worthDifference from best
1Sell Property A and reinvest$17,303,850—
2Rent out Property A$11,733,730$-5,570,120
3Live in Property A$10,411,360$-6,892,490

Why sell wins in the base case. Three things drive it, in order of size:

  1. The low assessment is worth less than the asset's opportunity cost. Prop 19 saves about $13,950 a year at the transfer date, rising with the 2% assessment cap. That is real, and it is the whole reason live-in beats rent-out. But it is a small number against a $3,100,000 asset.
  2. Property B already provides the housing. The heir can live in Property B's third unit without giving up a dollar of the $7,150 monthly rent, so living in Property A buys nothing that is not already owned — it only forfeits Property A's earning power.
  3. A stepped-up basis makes the sale tax cheap. Basis resets to date-of-death value, so a prompt sale produces almost no capital gain. Selling later converts appreciation into taxable gain and eventually loses the §121 shelter if the heir never occupies.

5. Estate and inheritance tax: the short version

  • California has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. No California estate tax return is required for any death on or after January 1, 2005 California State Controller's Office.
  • Inherited cash and property are not taxable income when received — federal or California California Franchise Tax Board.
  • The federal estate tax applies to the estate, not the heir, and only to the amount above $15,000,000 per decedent in 2026 Internal Revenue Service. A married couple with a portability election effectively gets twice that.
  • So the exposure is simply: does the estate exceed the exclusion? At the base-case 2045 values, the two properties alone are about $8,592,180. That leaves roughly $6,407,820 of headroom before the cash and other assets push the estate over $15,000,000.
  • The forward-indexation assumption is conservative. Every year after 2026 is modelled at the 2026 exclusion as a floor, because the IRS has not published future amounts. The real exclusion will be higher, so the modelled tax is an upper bound.

6. What to do, and in what order

  1. Get one actual Property B tax bill. It is the cheapest way to close the single largest data gap in this plan (the 2.14% implied rate).
  2. Confirm Property A is the parents' principal residence and is titled in the trust. Prop 19 turns entirely on the first; probate is avoided only if the second is true.
  3. Get insurance quotes for both properties, including an earthquake quote. This replaces the weakest number in the model with a real one.
  4. Decide the occupancy question before the death, not after. The one-year homeowners' exemption deadline and the three-year claim deadline are unforgiving, and the exclusion is worthless if nobody moves in.
  5. Re-run this model the year the valuation matters. The break-even reinvestment return is the number to watch.
