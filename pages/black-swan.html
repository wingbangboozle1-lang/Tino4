Read the table as a mechanism, not a forecast

Each shape is a straight-line decline of the published percentage over the published number of months, then either flat or a straight-line recovery to the prior high, with the reserve earning 3.5% a year. No dividends, fees, tax on the reserve, index spread or inflation adjustment are modeled. Parking money and buying after the bottom looks brilliant on a path that falls and stops falling, and worse than buying at once on a path that only rises: in the rising case a 36-month park costs about -$617,550 over ten years. The point is not which row wins; it is that staging is insurance paid for with expected return. Note also that the largest apparent gains in the table come from buying at the bottom of a straight-line path, which no one can identify while living through it: those rows bound what perfect timing would be worth, not what a household should expect.

What this build refuses to claim

No source read here forecasts the next fifty years: the budget office publishes a ten-year baseline with stated uncertainty, the price and house-price series report what already happened, and the history pages describe episodes. Anything on this site that looks like a long-run growth rate is a labelled scenario used to test a decision rule.
