STRESS TESTS

Black-swan conditions

Regime changes, not one smooth trend: what the official record shows and how to stress it.

Why one constant growth rate is the real error

A fifty-year projection built on one inflation rate and one return rate makes an unstable century look tidy. The published history contains inflation near 1 percent in 1964, more than 14 percent in 1980, and about 3.5 percent by the late 1980s; a 57 percent equity fall from 2007 to 2009; and an 89 percent index fall from 1929 that was not recovered until 1954. A model that averages those episodes into a single number cannot show the risk the household is actually exposed to.

How to build a fifty-year path honestly

Do not draw one smooth curve. Draw a sequence of regimes with explicit start years, and label each transition as an assumption. The published sources describe what happened and when; none of them forecasts this household, and the historical ranges are wider than most planning models assume. A defensible presentation shows the median path, the range across orderings, and the worst ordering, and it states which decisions stay sensible in all three.

Property-specific shocks that matter more than the macro path

A local rent ordinance, a special assessment, a seismic retrofit requirement, an insurer withdrawing from the area, a long vacancy, or a single large repair can move the household's outcome more than the difference between a 5 and 6 percent equity return. These are not tail events in a fifty-year window; they are ordinary operating risks that a macro stress test can hide.

Read the anchors correctly

The 57 percent figure describes the S&P 500 and the 89 percent figure describes the Dow Jones Industrial Average. They are different indexes measured over different periods, they are price indexes that exclude dividends, and neither is a probability. Using them as stress inputs is defensible; quoting them as expected losses is not.

What the regime simulator does

It walks a chosen sequence of regime blocks year by year, applies an optional one-year crash of a chosen depth in a chosen year, and compares investing the full amount immediately with staging the same amount into the market over 24 to 36 months while the balance sits in cash or Treasuries. It reports the inflation-adjusted ending value, the real annualised change, the largest peak-to-trough fall and when it happened, and a distribution of outcomes across a fixed number of seeded paths. Identical inputs always give identical results; nothing is random between runs and nothing leaves the browser.

Limits

The model applies a constant annual assumption inside each regime block and one instantaneous extra fall in the chosen year. It does not reconstruct a historical sequence, dividends, fees, taxes, contributions, withdrawals, property cash flows, changing Treasury yields, credit events, or the post-crash recovery path implied by the historical anchors. The 57 percent and 89 percent anchors come from different indexes (S&P 500 and Dow) measured over different periods; they are stress inputs, not probabilities and not comparable portfolio returns.

Reinvestment risk sits next to the headline

Staging softens crash-entry risk only while funds are still in the ladder: if a fall arrives after staging ends, both paths take the same hit. In exchange, staging accepts renewal risk on Treasury rungs, delayed market participation, and the possibility of trailing an immediate investment. Read both the drawdown line and the ending-value line together, and rerun with the fall moved to a different year.

Reading the phased path

A 24-month staged investor has roughly one eighth of the proceeds exposed when a crash lands in month 3, so the staged shock loss is roughly one eighth of the immediate loss; by month 25 the window is closed and both paths take the same hit. Staging trades that early protection for a rising-market lag and reinvestment risk on every rung. Reinvestment risk sits next to the headline.

The same average, two different outcomes

One million dollars of value grown at the block path ends at $2,038,730 after thirty years, against $2,038,730 on a flat 2.58% a year, even though the blocks average to that number. Any fifty-year projection built on a single rate is therefore not a forecast of this household's asset - it is a rounding of the risk away.

How this is meant to be used

Not as a probability. Each row is the same seven paths in a different economic world, priced with the same costs and the same tax rules. Where the winner changes, the household's job is to decide which world it can live with being wrong about, and to write that down before the appraisal and the tax figures arrive. Where the winner does not change, the decision is safe to make now.
