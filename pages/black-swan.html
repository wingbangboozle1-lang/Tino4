STRESS TESTS

Black-swan economic conditions

Historical evidence for uneven growth, inflation and market shocks, with strict limits on what those episodes can say about a future property or portfolio.

A single constant growth rate is not a 50-year economic model

Inflation, interest rates, housing prices, insurance availability, rents and equity returns move in different regimes. The available official outlook cited here is at most a near-term baseline; no official source supplies the future household-specific path.

Phased-investing path: Treasury staging before equity entry

Sale proceeds can be parked in short Treasuries across the 3-month bill to 2-year note band for 24–36 months after inheritance, then phased into stocks. This tests whether staging softens crash entry risk: a 1929-style equity collapse (Dow about 89 percent peak to trough, prior high not regained until November 1954) or a 2007–09-style collapse (S&P 500 about 57 percent peak to trough) beginning while money is still staged leaves part of the proceeds in Treasuries; the same collapse beginning after staging ends exposes the full amount. The local-only tool on the Treasury staging page compares crash-during-staging with crash-after-staging and runs lower Treasury renewal-yield cases.

Reinvestment risk beside the headline. Each maturing bill or note must be reinvested at the next auction's rate, which may be lower. Staging changes when equity risk begins; it does not remove reinvestment risk, sequence risk, or the possibility of stock losses.

Stress dimensions to combine

Test equity declines before, during and after Treasury staging; lower renewal yields; high inflation; stagnant housing prices; property-tax reassessment; insurance nonrenewal or coverage gaps; repair spikes; vacancy and rent limits; and liquidity needs. Treat joint shocks as stress cases, not independent probabilities. The comparison tool models entry timing plus user-entered yields, pre-crash drift and inflation; it does not claim to replay the whole economy.
