Decision analysis

Live in, rent out, or sell and reinvest — compared after tax on the same basis, with break-evens.

Recommendation: Sell Property A and reinvest

Terminal net worth $17,303,850 at 2065, which is $5,570,120 (47.5%) more than Rent out Property A. All three strategies are liquidated and taxed at the same horizon, so the comparison does not reward deferral.

The margin is large in the base case and it is not a sure thing. It depends most on the reinvestment return assumption. See the sensitivity section below.

Why each strategy wins or loses

Live in Property A — $10,411,360

Keeps the parent's low assessed value: the Prop 19 calculation preserves $1,044,590 of taxable value and saves $13,950 in the first year. But it turns a $3,100,000 asset into a place to sleep while Property B's third unit sits empty and available. It also costs the most in cash: negative $985,840 over the period, because Property B's rent does not cover both buildings' costs on the stated figures.

Rent out Property A — $11,733,730

Better than living in it, because the property earns. Worse than selling, because it is reassessed to market value — Prop 19 does not cover a rental home California State Board of Equalization — and the resulting tax bill is roughly $14,040 a year higher than the live-in case. Rent growth is also capped, and AB 1482 disappears in 2030 as written.

Sell & reinvest — $17,303,850

A stepped-up basis means an immediate sale is nearly tax-free — basis resets to $5,435,870 and the sale costs are deductible against it. The proceeds then compound without property tax, without a tenant, without a roof, and without the concentration risk of a single illiquid asset. This is the only strategy that produces material positive net cash ($551,700).

What all three share

Property B is held and rented in every scenario, so it contributes the same $3,611,400 to all three at the horizon. It is therefore not what separates them — it is the baseline. Its rent is also what makes living in Property A unnecessary rather than a sacrifice.

The break-even

The sell case gives up its lead only if the reinvestment return falls to roughly 4.5%. That figure is linearly interpolated between the two measured endpoints (4.5% and 8.5%), because the relationship is not exactly linear — annual tax is taken out of the portfolio each year. It is close enough to plan around and it is labelled rather than presented as exact.

A 4.5% return is a realistic outcome for a conservative portfolio. The Treasury curve on 2026-11-09 put the 10-year at 4.38%, the 20-year at 5.29% and the 30-year at 5.26% U.S. Department of the Treasury. If the money is invested mostly in bonds, the sell case is roughly a coin flip against living in the property. If a meaningful equity allocation is acceptable, it wins.

What this analysis does not capture

  • Being a landlord. No value is assigned to the work, the tenant risk, the eviction exposure, or the relocation payments a San Francisco owner pays to recover a unit — currently up to $24,733 per unit plus $5,497 per elderly or disabled tenant City and County of San Francisco.
  • Where the heir wants to live. The model assumes living in Property B's third unit is acceptable. If it is not, the live-in case is worth considerably more than shown, because the alternative is paying market rent somewhere.
  • Earthquake risk. Not modelled at all. Standard California policies exclude it and no premium was supplied.
  • Emotional value. A family home is not only an asset. Nothing here prices that, and nothing here should be read as telling anyone not to live in it.
  • Concentration. The sell case concentrates roughly $13,140,740 in securities, which carries its own risk that a single house does not.
