Comparison rule

Use the same transfer date, valuation date, after-tax horizon, inflation basis, and household housing need for every path. Show annual cash-flow ranges as well as terminal wealth, and label assumptions rather than presenting them as facts.

Write the rules before the results

Before any forecast arrives, document what would make the household choose each path: minimum liquidity, maximum acceptable maintenance risk, desired occupancy, rental obligations, tax eligibility, acceptable drawdown, and conditions that trigger a sale.

This page does not pick a path, and that is deliberate

Six competitive paths exist: occupy, rent out, sell in the transfer year, occupy then sell, a mixed structure, and sell then reinvest. A defensible ranking requires the same transfer date, the same valuation date, the same after-tax horizon, and evidence for every input each path depends on. Where that evidence is missing the path is marked blocked rather than estimated. The break-even page turns the largest of those unknowns into a single number you can go and obtain.

Model first: a sale in the transfer tax year

Model a sale in the inheritance tax year first, not as a tax-free assumption. Inherited basis is generally tied to date-of-death fair market value, but sale price, timing and allowable selling costs can differ from that value; a transfer-year sale does not automatically produce zero gain or loss. Verify the appraisal, amount realized and tax-year rules. Compare the after-tax proceeds with housing costs and reinvestment risk.

Ranges, not points

Replace every round-number assumption with a low, base and high band drawn from a written source: appraisal ranges, quoted insurance premiums, bid ranges, rent comparables, and published official thresholds. A model run on single numbers produces a false ranking; a model run on bands shows where the ranking is fragile. Where a band cannot be evidenced, leave the cell blank and flag it rather than filling it with a plausible number.
