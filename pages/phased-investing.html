STRESS TESTS

Treasury staging tool

Model 24–36 months of 3-month to 2-year Treasuries beside equity entry and renewal risk.

Headline and risk

Phasing sale proceeds into equities can reduce immediate exposure to an early fall, but can lag a rising market. Treasury interest resets at reinvestment; lower renewal yields, taxes, inflation, and delayed equity exposure can change the result.

Historical stress anchors

The calculator starts with the 57% anchor, which Federal Reserve History reports for the S&P 500 index from its October 2007 peak to its March 2009 trough: Federal Reserve History — The Great Recession. A 1929 stress run may use 89%, which the same official source reports for the Dow Jones Industrial Average from its September 1929 peak to the July 1932 close: Federal Reserve History — Stock Market Crash of 1929. These figures come from different indexes measured over different periods and are not comparable portfolio estimates, probabilities, or forecasts. A separate Federal Reserve History essay reports that home prices fell by over a fifth on average nationally from the first quarter of 2007 to the second quarter of 2011: Federal Reserve History — The Great Recession and Its Aftermath.

Parking proceeds in 3-month to 2-year Treasuries for 24–36 months

The staged path parks the undeployed balance in short Treasuries for the staging window, then buys equities on a fixed monthly schedule. Bills and notes are auctioned instruments with stated terms: a held-to-maturity holding differs from a sale before maturity, and each rung that matures inside the window must be reinvested at whatever yield then prevails. What the fixed scenarios show

Across the published set, at a 4% reserve yield and 7% drift, a 24-month window is ahead of a lump sum in 5 of 9 scenarios with a median advantage of 3.4% and a worst case of -4.7%. A 36-month window is ahead in 7 of 9 with a median of 6.3% and a worst case of -7.3%. The pattern is not 'staging wins'. It is that staging buys protection against a crash that arrives EARLY, and pays for it with the spread between equity and Treasury returns when the market simply rises. A crash that arrives as the window closes is close to a wash, and a crash after the window closes hits both paths almost equally. Reserved per the decision-log rule: these are assumption-driven outcomes at a single 60-month date, not probabilities, and they say nothing about any household's amounts.

The uncomfortable result worth keeping

In the 1929-shaped cases, staging helps but does not rescue the household. Even with a 36-month window, a 1929-shaped path leaves the staged portfolio roughly a third behind where a calm market would have put it. If the household cannot tolerate that, the answer is not a longer staging window; it is a smaller allocation to equities, a larger reserve, or a different decision about the property. No staging schedule converts a 25-year recovery into a safe one.

Limits of both tools on this page

The single-shock calculator above applies one instantaneous drawdown at a selected month with constant drift and yield otherwise. The scenario table and chart use a different, more realistic construction: the decline unfolds from the crash month to the anchor's trough over its recorded duration, then recovers to the prior peak over its recorded recovery period, and the reserve yield drops after the crash. Neither tool models dividends, fees, taxes, inflation, withdrawals, asset correlations, property cash flows, or price risk from selling a Treasury before maturity. In both, a 57% or 89% figure is a stress input taken from a named index, not a probability and not a forecast for any portfolio. The scenario results are assumption-driven and evaluated at a single 60-month date.

What the staging path is, in numbers

Park the net proceeds of a sale in a ladder of three-month to two-year Treasury maturities for 24 to 36 months after the transfer, then move into equities in equal tranches - twelve quarterly steps in the model. The point is not to beat the market; it is to stop a single decision date from deciding the next thirty years, and to pay for that insurance in a measurable way.

Reinvestment risk, next to the headline

Staging is not free. In a market that keeps rising the un-deployed money buys in higher: the model's own arithmetic gives $1,386,490 of price increase on the waiting money over the 30-month window at a 10 percent drift, and the waiting leg earns $64,660 in bills over the same span. In a crisis the safe leg earns close to nothing, because the rates on bills are cut in the same weeks the shares fall: the federal funds target went from 5.25 percent in September 2007 to 0 to 0.25 percent by December 2008, which is the reason a 2008-shaped crash is the best case for waiting and the worst case for the yield earned while waiting.

The decision rule this supports

Write the rule before the money exists: stage for a fixed window, buy on a fixed schedule, and do not extend the window because the market looks about to fall. A staging plan that is allowed to grow is a market-timing plan wearing a policy document, and the two rows above show what the discipline costs when nothing happens.

Mechanics worth fixing in advance

Bills are bought at auction through TreasuryDirect in denominations the site states, maturities run four, eight, thirteen, seventeen and twenty-six weeks for bills and two to ten years for notes, and interest on both is taxable federally and exempt from California income tax. A two-year note held to maturity returns par; sold early it can return less. None of that is a household fact and none of it needs to be guessed - it is read from the Treasury pages at the day of purchase.
