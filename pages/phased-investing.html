STRESS TESTS

Treasury staging and phased stock entry

A source-calibrated, assumption-led stress comparison for a 24–36 month 3M-to-2Y Treasury parking period followed by gradual equity purchases. It is not an individualized investment recommendation.

Reinvestment risk belongs beside the headline

Treasury bills and notes have fixed auction rates for their terms. When a short holding matures, the next auction yield may be lower; staging can therefore reduce early equity exposure yet leave more money earning less. A rising market can also make phased buying lag a lump-sum investment.

Crash-entry what-if — 3M to 2Y parking ladder, then phased stock entry

The parking period is 24 to 36 months after the inheritance, matching the plan under review: hold the proceeds in short Treasury paper, then phase into stocks. The starting portfolio is normalized to 100 index units so no household amount is embedded. The ladder split control sets how much of the parked money sits in 2-year notes, which lock a rate to their maturity, versus rolling 3-month bills, which re-price at each auction. Historical drawdown endpoints are sourced, but the path between peak and trough is synthetic and linear. Controls model a rising pre-crash market, separate first-year and renewal Treasury yields, an optional one-point fall in renewal yields, and an inflation-adjusted purchasing-power lens. Enter the Treasury yield net of estimated taxes and fees; the tool does not calculate taxes. This is not a historical total-return replay and it sends or stores no calculator inputs.

0% rates are neutral isolation settings, not Treasury quotes or forecasts.

Model boundary: equal monthly equity purchases from the initial 100 units; the parked reserve is split between 2-year notes and rolling 3-month bills in the proportion set by the split control; bills earn the entered yield in months 1–12 and the renewal yield afterwards, re-pricing every quarter; notes earn the entered yield in months 1–12 and the renewal yield afterwards and mature into the bill ladder at month 24; the final purchase includes any remaining reserve. Equity grows at the entered drift until the crash, declines linearly to the selected drawdown, then stays flat at its trough. Real units divide by the selected constant annual inflation factor. The reinvestment line prices a one-point fall in renewal yields (set by the stress control) and reports the difference between the two runs; notes still held when staging ends are assumed to be sold at par. There are no dividends, fees, taxes, post-trough recovery, withdrawals, property costs, correlations or probabilities. The zero-rate and zero-inflation defaults isolate timing only; they are not forecasts.

Interpretation

A staged plan can buy more shares during an early falling market because later installments purchase at lower index values. If the same decline starts after staging ends, all proceeds are already exposed. A positive pre-crash drift control tests one rising-market opportunity-cost scenario, but it is a constant synthetic rate rather than a historical return series. Use actual after-tax yields and an adviser-approved investment policy before making a real allocation.

How to use the ladder control

Set the split control to the proportion of the proceeds you would place in 2-year notes, which lock a rate to maturity, and the remainder in rolling 3-month bills, which re-price at each auction. A larger note share reduces how much of the parked money faces a renewal at an unknown rate; it also reduces how quickly the money is available if equities fall during the parking window. The reinvestment line under each headline prices the cost of a one-point fall in renewal yields so the headline is never read on its own.
